Galaxies are enormous systems made of stars, gas, dust, dark matter, and other objects.
The Milky Way is the galaxy that contains our Solar System.
Galaxies can contain millions, billions, or even trillions of stars.
Galaxies are commonly classified as spiral, elliptical, or irregular.
Spiral galaxies have rotating disks with curved arms extending from their centers.
Elliptical galaxies generally have smoother shapes and less visible cold gas than spiral galaxies.
Irregular galaxies do not have a clearly defined spiral or elliptical structure.
The Milky Way is a barred spiral galaxy.
The Andromeda Galaxy is the nearest major galaxy to the Milky Way.
Andromeda is located roughly 2.5 million light-years from Earth.
Light from Andromeda takes about 2.5 million years to reach us.
The Milky Way is about 100,000 light-years across.
A light-year measures distance, not time.
One light-year is the distance light travels in one year.
Light travels through space at about 300,000 kilometers per second.
The Milky Way contains hundreds of billions of stars.
The center of the Milky Way contains a supermassive black hole called Sagittarius A*.
Sagittarius A* has a mass of roughly four million Suns.
The Milky Way's central black hole is about 26,000 light-years from Earth.
Most large galaxies appear to have supermassive black holes in their centers.
Supermassive black holes can contain millions or billions of solar masses.
Galaxies are held together largely by gravity.
Dark matter contributes significantly to the gravitational structure of galaxies.
Dark matter does not emit, absorb, or reflect light in the way ordinary matter does.
The visible stars in a galaxy make up only part of its total mass.
Galaxies can collide with one another.
A galaxy collision does not usually mean that individual stars directly crash into each other.
The enormous distances between stars make direct stellar collisions rare during galaxy mergers.
Galaxy mergers can trigger bursts of new star formation.
The Milky Way and Andromeda are expected to merge in the distant future.
The Milky Way and Andromeda are currently moving toward each other because of gravity.
Galaxy mergers can dramatically reshape the appearance of the galaxies involved.
Some galaxies contain active galactic nuclei powered by matter falling toward a black hole.
Quasars are extremely luminous active galactic nuclei.
Some quasars can outshine the combined light of many billions of stars.
A galaxy can contain huge clouds of molecular gas where new stars are born.
Star-forming regions are often rich in gas and dust.
Supernova explosions can spread heavy elements throughout a galaxy.
Many elements heavier than hydrogen and helium were produced inside stars or during stellar explosions and mergers.
Galaxies recycle material between stars, gas clouds, and stellar remnants.
Old stars can return gas and enriched material to their surrounding galaxy.
Spiral arms are regions where star formation is often more active.
The Sun is located in the Milky Way's Orion Arm region.
The Solar System is not located near the center of the Milky Way.
The Sun takes roughly 230 million years to orbit the center of the Milky Way.
This period is sometimes called a galactic year.
The Milky Way rotates, but different parts of its disk orbit at different speeds.
The Milky Way has a central bulge surrounded by a broad stellar disk.
The Milky Way is surrounded by a much larger, faint stellar halo.
Globular clusters are dense groups of very old stars associated with galaxies.
The Milky Way has hundreds of known globular clusters.
Some galaxies have satellite galaxies orbiting them.
The Milky Way has several small satellite galaxies.
The Large Magellanic Cloud is a satellite galaxy of the Milky Way.
The Small Magellanic Cloud is another satellite galaxy associated with the Milky Way.
The Large Magellanic Cloud is roughly 160,000 light-years from Earth.
The Small Magellanic Cloud is roughly 200,000 light-years from Earth.
Some galaxies are so faint that they are difficult to detect even with powerful telescopes.
Dwarf galaxies can contain far fewer stars than giant galaxies.
Some dwarf galaxies contain only millions of stars.
Giant elliptical galaxies can contain hundreds of billions or more stars.
Some galaxies are almost entirely composed of old stars.
Some galaxies are extremely rich in young stars.
The colors of galaxies can reveal information about their stellar populations.
Bluish galaxies often contain many young, hot stars.
Reddish galaxies generally contain a larger proportion of older stars.
Dust can obscure the visible light coming from parts of a galaxy.
Infrared telescopes can reveal objects hidden behind some cosmic dust.
Radio telescopes can detect cold gas and energetic processes in galaxies.
X-ray telescopes can observe extremely hot gas and energetic black-hole environments.
Different wavelengths reveal different components of a galaxy.
Astronomers use galaxy spectra to determine chemical composition and physical conditions.
A galaxy's spectrum can also reveal how fast it is moving relative to Earth.
The expansion of the universe causes distant galaxies to appear redshifted.
The farther away many galaxies are, the faster they generally appear to recede because of cosmic expansion.
This relationship is known as Hubble's law or the Hubble–Lemaître law.
Some nearby galaxies can have blueshifted light because their local motion toward us can overcome cosmic expansion.
The Andromeda Galaxy is approaching the Milky Way and is therefore blueshifted.
Galaxy groups are collections of galaxies bound together by gravity.
The Milky Way belongs to the Local Group of galaxies.
The Local Group contains dozens of known galaxies.
The Local Group is part of a larger cosmic structure associated with the Virgo Supercluster region.
Galaxies are not distributed randomly throughout the universe.
They form enormous patterns known as the cosmic web.
The cosmic web contains filaments, sheets, clusters, and vast voids.
Galaxy clusters can contain hundreds or thousands of galaxies.
Galaxy clusters are among the largest gravitationally bound structures in the universe.
The space between galaxies in a cluster can contain extremely hot plasma.
Galaxy clusters can bend light through gravitational lensing.
Gravitational lensing occurs when massive objects distort the path of light.
A galaxy can act as a gravitational lens for a more distant galaxy.
Strong gravitational lensing can create multiple images of a distant galaxy.
Some gravitational lenses produce bright arcs around massive galaxy clusters.
Astronomers can use gravitational lensing to study otherwise difficult-to-see galaxies.
Very distant galaxies are observed as they existed billions of years ago.
Looking farther into space also means looking farther back in cosmic history.
The James Webb Space Telescope can observe some of the earliest known galaxies.
The Hubble Space Telescope has captured extremely deep views containing thousands of distant galaxies.
The Hubble Ultra Deep Field revealed thousands of galaxies within a tiny patch of sky.
Some galaxies seen in deep telescope images are billions of light-years away.
The observable universe contains an enormous number of galaxies.
Estimates of the number of galaxies have changed as telescopes have improved.
Galaxies observed today can differ greatly from their ancient counterparts.
Early galaxies were generally smaller and more irregular than many mature galaxies.
Small galaxies can merge to build larger galaxies over cosmic time.
Galaxy evolution is strongly influenced by mergers and interactions.
A galaxy can lose gas through powerful outflows.
Black-hole activity can drive enormous amounts of energy and material into a galaxy's surroundings.
These energetic outflows can influence future star formation.
Supernova-driven winds can also push gas out of galaxies.
Some galaxies contain enormous rings of gas and stars.
Some galaxies have unusually long tidal tails created by gravitational interactions.
Tidal tails can stretch hundreds of thousands of light-years from interacting galaxies.
The shape of a galaxy can change after a close encounter with another galaxy.
Elliptical galaxies generally have less organized rotation than spiral galaxy disks.
Some galaxies have very little ongoing star formation.
Galaxies with little star formation are sometimes described as quiescent galaxies.
Active galaxies can release enormous amounts of radiation from compact central regions.
Radio galaxies can produce giant radio-emitting structures extending far beyond their visible stars.
Jets from active supermassive black holes can travel enormous distances into intergalactic space.
Some black-hole jets move at speeds close to the speed of light.
A galaxy's central black hole can influence the gas and stars near its core.
The mass of a galaxy's central black hole is often related to properties of its central stellar bulge.
Galaxy rotation curves provide important evidence for the presence of dark matter.
Stars in the outer regions of many spiral galaxies orbit faster than visible matter alone would predict.
Dark matter helps explain why many galaxies remain gravitationally bound despite their observed rotation speeds.
Galaxies can contain planetary systems in addition to stars.
The Milky Way contains an enormous number of stars capable of hosting planets.
Astronomers have discovered planets orbiting stars outside the Milky Way, although detecting individual exoplanets in distant galaxies is extremely difficult.
Some galaxies contain huge reservoirs of neutral hydrogen gas.
Hydrogen is the most abundant element in the universe and a major component of galactic gas.
Molecular hydrogen clouds can collapse under gravity and form new stars.
The rate at which a galaxy forms stars can change dramatically over time.
Starbursts are galaxies or regions within galaxies experiencing exceptionally high rates of star formation.
Some galaxies are nicknamed starburst galaxies because of their intense stellar activity.
The Milky Way currently forms roughly one to a few new stars per year.
Galaxies can contain stellar remnants such as white dwarfs, neutron stars, and black holes.
Neutron stars can be created when massive stars explode as supernovae.
White dwarfs are remnants of stars similar in mass to the Sun.
Galactic magnetic fields can extend across enormous distances.
Cosmic rays travel through galaxies at very high energies.
Galaxies can contain enormous amounts of invisible dark matter surrounding their visible components.
The outer dark-matter halo of a galaxy can extend much farther than its visible disk.
Some galaxies have multiple nuclei, often indicating a recent or ongoing merger.

Merging supermassive black holes can eventually produce gravitational waves.

Astronomers can measure the ages of stellar populations within galaxies using their light.
The oldest stars in a galaxy can preserve clues about its early history.

Galaxies generally become more chemically enriched as successive generations of stars evolve.
Gas flowing into galaxies can provide material for future star formation.
Galaxies can exchange material with the surrounding intergalactic medium.
The boundary between a galaxy and intergalactic space is not always sharply defined.
Some galaxies have extremely compact centers packed with stars.
Ultra-diffuse galaxies can be surprisingly large while containing relatively few stars.
Galaxy clusters can contain dark matter far exceeding the mass of their visible galaxies.
The gravitational influence of a galaxy can affect nearby smaller galaxies.
Satellite galaxies can experience tidal forces from their larger host galaxies.
Tidal forces can gradually strip stars and gas from small galaxies.
A galaxy's environment can influence how quickly it forms new stars.
Dense galaxy clusters can contain many galaxies that have stopped forming stars efficiently.
Astronomers study galaxies to understand how structure formed in the universe.
Galaxy formation began relatively soon after the universe became transparent to light.
The earliest galaxies formed from matter that had gathered under gravity in the young universe.
Tiny density differences in the early universe eventually helped grow large cosmic structures.
Gravity causes dark matter and ordinary matter to gather into increasingly complex structures.
Galaxies are still evolving today.
The universe contains galaxies with vastly different sizes, ages, shapes, and histories.
